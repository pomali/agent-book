# What a fastener really does

Put two books flat on a table, one on top of the other, and push the top one
sideways. It slides. Now press down hard on the top book with your other hand
and push again. It does not move, or it drags the bottom book with it.

You have just built a bolted joint. Your hand was the bolt.

Notice what your hand did not do. It did not block the book. It was not a peg
standing in the way. It only pressed, and the pressing made friction, and the
friction did the holding.

This is the idea the rest of the book stands on. A screw or a bolt is not
mainly a pin that parts hang on. It is a **clamp**. It squeezes parts
together, and the squeeze is called **clamp load**. When something tries to
slide the parts, friction between them says no, and the amount of friction
available is simply the clamp load times how grippy the surfaces are. For dry
steel on steel that grip is about 0.15 to 0.2: every 1000 newtons of squeeze
buys you 150 to 200 newtons of resistance to sliding.

## The bolt is a spring

Where does the squeeze come from? From stretch.

When you tighten a nut, the first turns just bring things into contact. After
that, every further bit of rotation tries to make the bolt shorter than the
stack of parts it passes through. It cannot be, so the bolt stretches. Not by
much: a few hundredths of a millimetre on a small bolt. But steel is stiff,
and a tiny stretch means a large force. The bolt is now a very stiff spring,
pulled tight and trying to get back to its own length, and the only way back
is to squeeze the parts between its head and its nut.

The tension sitting in the bolt is called **preload**. It is there before any
outside force arrives, which is where the name comes from. With nothing else
pulling on the joint, preload and clamp load are the same number seen from two
sides: the bolt is pulled, the parts are pressed.

This picture explains things that otherwise look like folklore:

- **Why a slightly loose bolt is far worse than a tight one.** With no clamp
  load there is no friction. Now the parts do slide, they hammer against the
  shank of the bolt with every vibration, and the bolt is doing a job it was
  not shaped for. A joint that keeps its clamp load keeps its strength. Most
  failures begin on the day the clamp load went away, long before anything
  broke.
- **Why long thin bolts are sometimes chosen on purpose.** A longer spring
  loses less force when the thing it presses on settles by a hair. We come
  back to this in part 07.
- **Why "how tight" is a real question with a real answer.** Too little and
  the joint slides. Too much and the spring is stretched past the point where
  it springs back. Part 06 is about finding the middle.

## And in wood?

A wood screw also clamps. Drive one through a batten into a post and you can
watch the gap close and the batten bite. The difference is what happens next.
Steel stays where you put it. Wood is alive in a slow way: it swells and
shrinks with the seasons and gives a little under constant pressure. The clamp
load you made on Saturday fades. So in wood the body of the screw, its grip in
the fibres and its resistance to bending, carries more of the work over the
years than a bolt in a steel joint ever should.

That is why the wood chapters care so much about holes and grain, and the
metal chapters care so much about tightness. Same principle; different
material remembering it differently.

## The one sentence

**A fastener makes clamp load; clamp load makes friction; friction holds the
joint.** If you remember nothing else, remember that the bolt's first job is
to squeeze.

## Try it

1. The two books, as above. Then put a sheet of baking paper between them and
   repeat. Same squeeze, less grip: that is the "coefficient of friction".
2. Find any bolted thing nearby: a chair, a bike stem, a shelf bracket. Ask:
   which surfaces are being pressed together? Where would it slide if the
   squeeze vanished?

## Checks

- "If the bolt only squeezes, what actually stops the bracket sliding down
  the wall?" (friction between bracket and wall, made by clamp load)
- "Why is a bolt that has loosened half a turn in danger even though it is
  still in the hole?" (no preload → no friction → parts move and load the
  shank)
- "Where does the squeezing force come from?" (the bolt stretching like a
  spring)

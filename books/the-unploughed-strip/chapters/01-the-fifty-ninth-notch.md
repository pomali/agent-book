# The Fifty-Ninth Notch

Baba woke her by the foot.

It was dark in the house and warm from the oven. Jakub was asleep on the
bench with his arm over his face, and the baby breathed in the cradle like a
small bellows. Katka found her shoes by feel and did not put them on until
she was outside, because the threshold creaked and Baba had stepped over it
without a sound.

"Why not Jakub?" she asked in the yard.

"Because I woke you," said Baba. She had a cloth bundle under her arm and
something long in her other hand that was not her stick.

They went down between the strips. There was no moon, but the path was pale
and Katka had walked it every day of her life. The fallow lay on their left,
last year's stubble gone grey and the new grass coming through it. At the
bottom, where the family's land ran out against the wood, the ground rose in
a long low hump, dark with hazel and thorn. Six paces wide. Katka had counted
it more than once. Nobody had ever told her to stay off the Share, and she
had never set foot on it.

Baba did not set foot on it either. She went round by the end and into the
alders.

It was colder under the trees. The stream ran somewhere to the right, quick
and shallow, and then the sound of it changed and went quiet, and Katka knew
they had come to the Black Eye before she could see it. There was a little
light on the water that did not seem to come from the sky.

Baba unwrapped the cloth. It held the heel of a loaf, hard as a hoof.

"That's from Easter," said Katka.

"It's from the first furrow. Last autumn's sowing, the first sheaf, the
first flour, the first loaf. You'll bake it yourself next year, and you'll
keep the heel where the mice can't reach." Baba put the bread in her hands.
"Go to the edge. Put it on the water. Don't throw it. Then watch it, and tell
me what it does."

"Aren't you coming?"

Baba had already turned her back. She stood facing up the path, the way they
had come, like someone waiting for a cart.

"If I look, it floats," she said. "Go on."

The bank was soft. Katka knelt, and leaned, and set the bread on the water
the way you set a bowl on a table. It rocked. It turned once, slowly. The
stream came in at the top of the pool and went out at the bottom, and
anything that fell in the Black Eye went round by the left bank and out
between the two stones; she had seen leaves do it a hundred times, and a
duckling once.

The bread went the other way. It moved up the pool, against the water, as
far as the middle, where it was deepest. It stopped there. Then it went down.
It did not get wet and heavy and sink. It went down all at once and level,
like a thing lifted off a shelf from below.

There was a ring on the water, and then there was not.

Katka stayed on her knees. On the far bank, among the roots, something
dripped, steadily, as if a coat had been hung up there. She did not look at
it. She was not sure afterwards whether she had decided not to.

"Well?" said Baba, without turning.

"It went down. In the middle." Her voice came out ordinary. "It went up the
pool first."

"Good." Baba let her breath go. "Come here."

The long thing in her hand was a hazel rod, peeled, grey with handling, as
long as Katka's arm. One side of it was cut with notches from end to end.
Baba took the small knife from her belt and found the place by thumb, and cut
a new one, two strokes, and blew the shaving away.

"Count them."

There was just enough light. Katka ran her nail down the stick. She counted
the way she counted geese, in fives, with her lips.

"Fifty-nine."

"Fifty-nine. I cut the first one when I was ten years old and my mother held
my hand on the knife." Baba wrapped the stick in the cloth the bread had come
in. "Now you've seen it. That's all it takes. It's yours when I'm done with
it."

"What's it for?"

"It's what's been paid."

"Paid for what? How much is there to pay?"

But that was two questions, and Baba was already walking.

By the time the sun was on the Upper field the whole village was in the
lane. St George's was the day the cattle went out, and every beast in
Lieskovany had green birch tied to its horns. Ondro had the oxen for the
first time. He had been given a switch and told to be master of them, and he
was holding it like a candle.

Sivko went where he was pointed. Rohan went where Sivko went, after thinking
about it. They came down onto the fallow and spread out with the neighbours'
cows, heads down, tearing at the new grass, and worked their way along the
strips to the bottom.

At the edge of the Share, both oxen stopped. There was good grass on the
hump, better than on the fallow. Rohan stretched his neck toward it and blew.
Then he turned along the edge and grazed away from it, and Sivko followed.

"They always do that," said Ondro, to nobody. "Lyska too."

Jakub had come down behind them. He walked along the foot of the Share,
slowly, with long even steps, and at the far end he stood and looked back up
it. Katka knew what he was doing because she had done it herself.

"Two hundred," he said when he reached her. "By six. You know what that is?
That's four sacks of rye. Every year. Sixty years."

"Fifty-nine," said Katka.

He looked at her.

"Since when do you know?"

She did not answer. She was watching the wood. A man had come out of the
alders at the far corner, a stranger in a short town coat, and behind him a
boy carrying a coil of knotted cord over his shoulder. The man drove a peg
into the ground with his heel, where the Share ended and the trees began, and
the boy began to pay out the cord.

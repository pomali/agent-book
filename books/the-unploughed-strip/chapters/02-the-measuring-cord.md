# The Measuring Cord

It did not rain in May.

The oats on the Stony came up thin and stood still. Agneša's flax, which
wanted wet feet, got water carried to it in the evenings, two pails at a
time, until Katka's shoulders knew the weight of the yoke without her. The
sky was high and pale and the dust in the lane was as fine as flour.

For Rusadlá the houses were dressed with green boughs all the same, and
Father Hermann walked up from the lower village to say mass in the chapel.
Afterwards the men stayed under the linden, because the šoltýs had asked
them to.

Kunrat stood to speak, which nobody in Lieskovany did. He was a broad, tidy
man with a grey beard cut short, and he spoke their tongue correctly and
slowly, like someone carrying a full dish. Trudka stood a little behind him
in case a word was missing. Jakub had found a place where he could see her.

"I have my lord's charter three years," Kunrat said. "You have seen what is
done up the valley. Eleven families come at St Michael, good people. They
will want land. Under the charter, the waste of this valley is mine to give
them. I do not take what is any man's. I say this plainly so that there is
no quarrel later."

"What's waste?" said old Pavol.

"Land that is not ploughed, not mown, and not built on."

"The wood?"

"The wood. The alders, and the wet ground below. It is good soil under
there. Drained, it is the best in the valley."

Nobody said anything. Somebody's dog scratched itself. Katka, on the chapel
step with the baby, saw her father look at the ground between his feet, and
saw Baba, on the women's side, look at nothing at all with great attention.

"And the strip," Kunrat said. He was not unkind about it. "At the foot of
Matej's land. My man has measured it. Two hundred paces by six, in thorn. I
ask, because I do not know your custom: is that ploughed land, or is it
waste?"

Matej took his time.

"It's ours," he said.

"I don't doubt it was. I ask if it is used."

"It's used."

"For what?"

That was the question. Katka watched her father not find the answer. He was
an honest man and he would not say *for hay*, when it had never been mown
before St John, or *for grazing*, when no beast would touch it. The pause
grew until it was itself a kind of answer.

It was Father Hermann who ended it. He had been listening with his head on
one side, as he did.

"Perhaps," he said, "it can wait for the autumn. Nobody is settling anybody
before St Michael, and nobody is ploughing thorn in a drought." He smiled at
Kunrat, and then, a little longer, at Baba. "I should like to understand the
custom myself. I am told there is one."

"There's a hedge," said Baba. "It keeps the cattle out of the wood."

"A hedge six paces wide?"

"They're big cattle."

Blažej the miller laughed, and then several people did, and the meeting
broke the way a clod breaks. But going home, Jakub walked beside his father
instead of behind him.

"He's right, though," Jakub said. "By his paper he's right. If it's not
ploughed, it's waste, and if it's waste, it's his, and then it's some
stranger's rye growing at the bottom of our land. Not ours. Not theirs
either." He nodded toward the alders without looking at them. "Whoever
they are, they'll have a Saxon's plough through it. Is that better?"

"Leave it," said Matej.

"I'm asking what we get. Fifty-nine years. What do we get for it?"

"We're here," said Baba, from behind them.

"So is everyone else, and they plough to the edge."

Baba did not answer that. Katka, carrying Hana, thought that it sounded like
a thing with an answer, and that Baba was holding the answer shut, the way
you hold a door against wind.

That evening it was her turn to milk. Lyska gave what she could on dry
grass, and it was little, and Agneša had said that morning that the dish by
the threshold could go without for once, since the child needed it more than
a snake did.

Katka stood in the doorway with the pail. The dish was there in its place
under the lip of the sill, a shallow wooden thing worn pale in the middle.
It was dry. There was a line of dust across it.

She had never seen the Householder. Jakub said he had, once: a length of
something the colour of a peeled stick, going under the stone. Ondro said he
saw it every week, but Ondro said that about wolves.

She thought about four sacks of rye. She thought about bread going the wrong
way up a pool.

She poured the dish full, quickly, not looking round, and took the rest
inside.

In the morning it was empty and licked clean, and there was no dust on it at
all. That proved nothing. There were cats.

# Canon: Ajax

Binding for the agent. The play's text outranks everything here: if an item
below seems to contradict a scene file, the scene is right and this file has a
bug.

## S: The text

- S1. *Ajax* is by Sophocles, an Athenian (about 496–406 BCE). Its date is not
  recorded. It is usually thought to be among the earliest of his seven
  surviving plays, perhaps from the 440s BCE.
- S2. The English here is Francis Storr's verse translation of 1913. Its
  diction was old-fashioned on purpose even then.
- S3. The Greek text has no stage directions. Every direction here is Storr's
  inference.
- S4. Dialogue in the Greek is in spoken verse; choral odes and moments of
  high emotion are sung. Storr marks this by changing metre and rhyme.
- S5. Speeches are cited `scene.speech`. These numbers belong to this edition
  only.

## B: Before the play (what the first audience already knew)

- B1. The Greeks have besieged Troy for about ten years under Agamemnon and
  his brother Menelaus, the sons of Atreus ("the Atridae").
- B2. Ajax, son of Telamon, from the island of Salamis, is the greatest Greek
  fighter after Achilles. He is huge, steady, a defender: his emblem is a
  great shield of seven oxhides. In Homer he is the one major hero who is
  never helped in battle by a god. When Achilles fell, it was Ajax who
  carried his body out of the fighting.
- B3. Ajax once fought Hector, the Trojan champion, in single combat until
  nightfall. They parted with gifts: Hector gave Ajax a sword, Ajax gave
  Hector a belt.
- B4. Achilles is dead. His armour was to go to the best of the Greeks. Ajax
  and Odysseus both claimed it; it was awarded to Odysseus. In this play the
  award was made by a vote of judges; Ajax and Teucer say the vote was
  rigged by the Atridae, and Menelaus denies it. Other ancient tellings name
  other judges.
- B5. The night before the play, Ajax set out alone to kill Agamemnon,
  Menelaus and Odysseus. Athena turned his mind so that he fell instead on
  the army's captured cattle and sheep, and their herdsmen, believing them
  to be his enemies. He killed some and dragged others to his hut to torture.
- B6. Tecmessa is the daughter of Teleutas, a Phrygian king. Ajax sacked her
  city and took her as his captive; she lives with him as his wife and has
  borne him a son, Eurysaces ("broad shield"), still a small child.
- B7. Teucer is Ajax's half-brother: Telamon's son by Hesione, a Trojan
  princess, daughter of King Laomedon, given to Telamon as a prize of war.
  He is the best archer in the army. His enemies call him a slave's son.
- B8. The Chorus are sailors from Salamis who came to Troy with Ajax. They
  depend on him entirely. They claim kinship with Athens.
- B9. Athena's anger, as the seer Calchas reports it in scene 06: when Ajax
  left home his father told him to seek victory with the gods' help, and he
  answered that with the gods even a nobody could win, and he meant to win
  without them. Later, in battle, he told Athena to go and stand by the
  other Greeks, since the line would never break where he stood.
- B10. Odysseus is Athena's favourite and Ajax's particular enemy. Ajax's
  party calls him a son of Sisyphus, the trickster, instead of Laertes.
- B11. Telamon, the father, took part in an earlier sack of Troy and came
  home with the highest prize. Ajax measures himself against that.
- B12. Ajax's mother is Eriboea.

## P: What happens

- P1. (01) Odysseus tracks the killer to Ajax's hut. Athena tells him what
  happened, calls the still-deluded Ajax out, and shows him off. Ajax
  believes Odysseus is tied up inside awaiting the whip. Odysseus pities him.
- P2. (02) The sailors arrive, hoping the rumour is false. Tecmessa tells
  them the night's events. Ajax, now sane, is heard crying out.
- P3. (03) The hut is opened and Ajax is seen among the slaughtered animals.
  He laments, then reasons his way to the conclusion that he must die.
- P4. (04) Tecmessa pleads with him to live. He sends for his son, gives him
  the shield, leaves instructions for Teucer, and has the door shut.
- P5. (05) Ajax comes out with the sword and says he has softened: he will go
  to the shore, cleanse himself, bury the sword, and learn to yield. The
  Chorus rejoices.
- P6. (06) A messenger reports Teucer's return, the army's hostility, and
  Calchas's warning that Ajax must be kept indoors for this one day. Tecmessa
  and the Chorus go to search.
- P7. (07) Alone on the shore, Ajax fixes Hector's sword in the ground, prays,
  curses, says farewell, and falls on it.
- P8. (08) The Chorus returns in two halves. Tecmessa finds the body and
  covers it with her cloak.
- P9. (09) Teucer comes, sends for the child, and laments. Menelaus forbids
  burial; they quarrel; Menelaus leaves to fetch force. Teucer sets the child
  by the body as a suppliant and goes to find a grave.
- P10. (10) Agamemnon repeats the ban and insults Teucer's birth; Teucer
  answers in kind. Odysseus intervenes and persuades Agamemnon to allow
  burial. Teucer accepts his friendship, declines his help with the body, and
  leads the funeral.

## D: The theatre

- D1. First performed in Athens at a festival of Dionysus: outdoors, by
  daylight, before many thousands, all performers male and masked.
- D2. Three actors played all the speaking parts between them. The Chorus
  numbered twelve or fifteen, sang and danced in a circular space (the
  orchestra), and was accompanied by a reed pipe, the aulos.
- D3. Behind the playing space stood a wooden building with a central door,
  here Ajax's hut. A wheeled platform could be rolled out of the door to show
  an interior scene; many scholars think the reveal in scene 03 used it.
- D4. A chorus normally stays in view from its entrance to the end. In *Ajax*
  it leaves mid-play and the scene changes to another place. Both are very
  rare in surviving tragedy.
- D5. Violent death in Greek tragedy is almost always reported, not shown.
  Ajax's death happens on stage or just out of sight; how it was staged is
  not known and is still argued about.
- D6. Whether Athena appeared above the building or on the ground in scene 01
  is debated. Storr's direction puts her above.
- D7. A common reconstruction of the casting: first actor Ajax, then Teucer;
  second actor Odysseus and Tecmessa; third actor Athena, the Messenger,
  Menelaus and Agamemnon. If so, when Tecmessa returns in scene 09 she is
  played by a silent extra, which is why she never speaks again.
- D8. Odes are built in matched pairs, strophe and antistrophe, sung to the
  same music and dance.

## A: After and around the play

- A1. In Homer's *Odyssey* (book 11) Odysseus meets the ghost of Ajax among
  the dead, speaks to him with regret about the armour, and gets no answer.
  Ajax turns and walks away.
- A2. Ajax was not only a character to the first audience. He was a hero who
  received worship at Athens and Salamis, and one of the ten tribes into
  which Athenian citizens were divided, Aiantis, was named after him. His
  tomb was shown near Troy.
- A3. To leave a body unburied was the worst dishonour and an offence against
  the gods. Sophocles returns to a forbidden burial in *Antigone*.
- A4. In later tradition Telamon does drive Teucer out for coming home
  without his brother, and Teucer founds a new Salamis in Cyprus.
- A5. A famous Athenian vase by the painter Exekias, about a century older
  than the play, shows Ajax alone, planting the sword. The image was well
  known before Sophocles wrote.

## Q: Open questions (do not settle; see BOOK.md rule 4)

- Q1. **The speech about time (scene 05).** Is Ajax deliberately deceiving
  Tecmessa and the Chorus so that he can get away? Speaking truths in a form
  they will mishear? Or has he really come to understand yielding and change,
  and decided he cannot live in such a world? The text supports each.
- Q2. **Two plays or one?** The hero dies a little past halfway; the rest is
  a quarrel over his burial. Ancient and modern critics have called it
  broken-backed. Others say the burial is the point: the play is about what
  Ajax is worth, and that is only decided at the end.
- Q3. **Athena.** Just punishment of arrogance, or cruelty? She saves three
  men's lives by maddening Ajax, and then invites his enemy to laugh.
- Q4. **Why Odysseus?** Pity, justice, piety, or the prudence of a man who
  knows he will need a grave himself? He gives all of these reasons.
- Q5. **Is Ajax admirable?** He meant to murder his commanders in their
  sleep and never repents of it. The play ends by calling him the noblest of
  men.
- Q6. **Tecmessa.** Does anything she says reach him? Some hear her appeal in
  the pity he admits to in scene 05.
